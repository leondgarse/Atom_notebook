## Week 1 Summary
- **Simple Linear Regression Foundation 简单线性回归基础**
  - **The PRF and OLS:** The Population Regression Function is defined as E[y|x] = B0 + B1*x. We estimate the parameters (B0_hat and B1_hat) using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), which works by minimizing the Sum of Squared Residuals (SSR).
  - **Zero Conditional Mean Assumption:** E[u|x] = 0 is the most critical assumption for isolating causality. It dictates that the average value of all unobserved factors in the error term (u) must be zero for any specific, given value of x.
  - **Omitted Variable Bias:** If an unobserved factor (like innate intelligence in an education-wage model, or building quality in a lease-price model) is correlated with the independent variable x, it violates the zero conditional mean assumption and prevents a clean causal interpretation.
  - **PRF与OLS：** 总体回归函数定义为 E[y|x] = B0 + B1*x。我们使用普通最小二乘法 (OLS) 来估计参数（B0_hat 和 B1_hat），其工作原理是最小化残差平方和 (SSR)。
  - **零条件均值假设：** E[u|x] = 0 是隔离因果关系最关键的假设。它规定对于任何特定的给定 x 值，误差项 (u) 中所有未观察到因素的平均值必须为零。
  - **遗漏变量偏差：** 如果未观察到的因素（如教育-工资模型中的先天智力，或租期-价格模型中的建筑质量）与自变量 x 相关，就会违反零条件均值假设，从而无法得出清晰的因果解释。
- **Goodness of Fit and Data Scaling 拟合优度与数据缩放**
  - **R-squared (R^2):** A statistical measure representing the proportion of the variance for a dependent variable that's explained by an independent variable. Calculated as SSE/SST (or 1 - SSR/SST). A higher R^2 means a better statistical fit, but it does not automatically imply a valid causal model.
  - **Changing Units of Measurement:** Scaling the data changes the mathematical coefficient but not the economic meaning. Multiplying the x variable by a constant requires dividing the slope parameter (B1) by that exact same constant to maintain mathematical equality.
  - **R平方 (R^2)：** 一种统计量度，表示因变量的方差中能由自变量解释的比例。计算公式为 SSE/SST（或 1 - SSR/SST）。较高的 R^2 意味着更好的统计拟合度，但这并不自动意味着它是一个有效的因果模型。
  - **改变测量单位：** 缩放数据会改变数学系数，但不会改变经济学含义。将 x 变量乘以一个常数，需要将斜率参数 (B1) 除以完全相同的常数，以保持数学等式不变。
- **Logarithmic Transformations 对数转换**
  - **Log-Level Model:** Regressing log(y) on x. The coefficient B1 multiplied by 100 represents the exact percentage change in y for a one-unit absolute increase in x.
  - **Log-Log Model (Elasticity):** Regressing log(y) on log(x). The coefficient B1 is interpreted directly as an elasticity. A 1% change in x is associated with a B1% change in y. Do not multiply this coefficient by 100.
  - **对数-水平模型：** 将 log(y) 对 x 进行回归。系数 B1 乘以 100 表示 x 绝对增加一个单位时，y 的精确百分比变化。
  - **对数-对数模型（弹性）：** 将 log(y) 对 log(x) 进行回归。系数 B1 直接被解释为弹性。x 发生 1% 的变化，y 就会发生 B1% 的变化。不要将此系数乘以 100。
- **Statistical Properties of OLS Estimators OLS估计量的统计性质**
  - **Estimator as a Random Variable:** Because the OLS estimator is calculated from random sample data (X and Y), the estimator itself is a random variable with its own probability distribution, mean, and variance.
  - **Unbiasedness:** Requires four assumptions: linearity in parameters, random sampling, positive variance in x, and zero conditional mean. When satisfied, the expected value of the estimator strictly equals the true population parameter across infinite samples.
  - **Variance and Homoscedasticity:** Homoscedasticity assumes the variance of the error term is completely constant and independent of x. A larger variation in your x variable sample actually improves the efficiency of your model by drastically reducing the variance of the estimator.
  - **作为随机变量的估计量：** 因为 OLS 估计量是根据随机样本数据（X 和 Y）计算得出的，所以估计量本身就是一个随机变量，拥有自己的概率分布、均值和方差。
  - **无偏性：** 需要满足四个假设：参数呈线性、随机抽样、x 的方差为正、以及零条件均值。当满足这些条件时，在无限样本中，估计量的期望值严格等于真实的总体参数。
  - **方差与同方差性：** 同方差性假设误差项的方差是完全恒定的，且独立于 x。x 变量样本中更大的变异实际上会通过大幅降低估计量的方差来提高模型的有效性。
## Week 1 Learning Tips
Welcome back to another challenging but rewarding week of econometrics! As you review the material on Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), I want you to focus heavily on the "why" behind the mathematical mechanics. A common pitfall I see students make is confusing a "good fit" (a high R-squared) with a "good causal model." Remember, R-squared simply tells you how tightly your data points hug the regression line; it is a measure of variance explained. However, a model with a massive R-squared can still suffer severely from omitted variable bias if the zero conditional mean assumption (E[u|x] = 0) is violated. Never let a high R-squared blind you to a flawed theoretical foundation. When you evaluate a regression, always ask yourself: "What factors have been pushed into the error term, and are they secretly correlated with my independent variable?"

欢迎回到充满挑战但收获满满的计量经济学新一周！当你们复习普通最小二乘法 (OLS) 的材料时，我希望你们能把重点放在数学机制背后的“为什么”上。我常看到学生犯的一个陷阱，就是把“良好的拟合度”（高R平方）与“良好的因果模型”混为一谈。记住，R平方只是告诉你数据点有多么紧密地贴合回归线；它是对已解释方差的衡量。然而，如果违反了零条件均值假设 (E[u|x] = 0)，一个拥有极高R平方的模型仍然可能遭受严重的遗漏变量偏差。千万不要让高R平方蒙蔽了你对理论基础缺陷的洞察力。当你在评估一个回归模型时，永远要问自己：“哪些因素被推入了误差项，它们是否在暗中与我的自变量存在相关性？”

Another critical area to master is interpreting logarithmic transformations, which is notoriously tricky for beginners. The intuition here is entirely about multiplicative vs. additive changes. When we use a log-log model (like CEO salary against company sales), we are stripping away raw units (like dollars) and looking purely at proportional relationships, which we call elasticity. The key pitfall to avoid is mechanically multiplying everything by 100. If you are dealing with a log-log model, the coefficient is already the elasticity—a 1% change in X leads to a B1% change in Y. You only multiply by 100 in a log-level model, where the independent variable is still moving in raw, absolute units (like one year of education). To master this, I highly recommend creating a simple cheat sheet with three columns: Functional Form (Level-Level, Log-Level, Log-Log), the Math Derivative, and the standard English interpretation sentence structure. Practice verbally explaining these coefficients out loud as if you were presenting them in a boardroom; speaking the interpretation cements the logic far better than just reading the formula.

另一个需要掌握的关键领域是解释对数转换，这对初学者来说出了名地棘手。这里的直觉完全在于乘积变化与加性变化之间的区别。当我们使用对数-对数模型（如CEO薪水对公司销售额）时，我们剥离了原始单位（如美元），纯粹观察比例关系，我们称之为弹性。要避免的关键陷阱是机械地将所有东西都乘以100。如果你处理的是对数-对数模型，系数本身就已经代表了弹性——X变化1%会导致Y变化B1%。你只有在对数-水平模型中才需要乘以100，因为在这个模型中，自变量仍然是以原始的绝对单位（如一年教育）变动的。为了掌握这一点，我强烈建议大家制作一个简单的备忘小抄，分为三列：函数形式（水平-水平、对数-水平、对数-对数）、数学导数、以及标准的英文解释句式。试着大声练习口头解释这些系数，就像你在董事会会议上做报告一样；说出你的解释，能比仅仅阅读公式更好地巩固这一逻辑。

    Okay, I think we should clarify this further because there is a difference between conditional and unconditional expectations. When an expectation is unconditional, it is purely affected by the entire distribution. So, when you have a variable that can be expressed with a constant, say 5x, the expected value E[5x] is simply equal to 5 times the expected value of x, E[x]. So if E[x] is 1, the result is 5. But the situation we are dealing with right now is slightly different because we are talking about conditional expectations. Let us do a quick calculation here. Remember, when you compute the conditional expectation, you have to ask which goes first. Here, the calculation is: what is the expected value of y conditional on x being equal to zero? When you calculate this expectation, you already know the specific value of x. In this case, x is already given as zero. So what you end up with is an equation where x is replaced by zero, leaving you with just the constant term plus the error term, u, because the x variable has already been fixed to zero. That is the fundamental difference between unconditional and conditional. When you do the unconditional expectation, x can be anything across its entire distribution. But when we do the conditional expectation, x is given that particular, fixed value. Differentiating between conditional and unconditional expectations is vitally important for econometrics, and that is why we are spending time doing this drill.

  - [18:40:41 - 18:43:32] **Introduction to Simple Linear Regression**

    Now we transition back to our formal discussion, which is the introduction to regression. For this specific chapter, we are focusing exclusively on the simple linear regression model. We need to discuss how to interpret the coefficients and how we mathematically derive the estimates using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), alongside understanding the underlying properties of these estimates. The basic definition is pretty simple. We have one outcome variable, which is our dependent variable Y, and one independent variable, which we sometimes call the control variable or the regressor, X. We use this model when we try to analyze how changes in X go on to affect Y. A classic example could be predicting the final grade in your exam based on the number of hours you study for the whole term or every week. While that specific relationship is pretty clear, a single variable almost never can fully explain the entirety of the outcome. You cannot explain all the variations in exam grades just with study hours, because everyone has factors they cannot control—like feeling sick on exam day, innate ability, or even external noise. Whatever factors you cannot control for will inherently go into the error term, which we call u. If you do not have that error term, you cannot establish that mathematical equality. That is why you cannot just write a general deterministic equation like y = β0 + β1 * x without u. When considering the interpretation, you have to think about the marginal contribution of x. We do this by taking the partial derivative: partial y with respect to partial x. When taking this derivative, we are implicitly assuming the concept we talked about last week: ceteris paribus. This means holding all other factors constant. If I do a pushup, how many calories do I burn? I don't know exactly, but if I evaluate it, I have to hold my current weight, age, and all other variables constant, and only look at the one-level change in pushups. What happens when you hold everything else constant and only change x? That isolation is exactly what potential β1 captures.

  - [18:43:32 - 18:50:09] **The Zero Conditional Mean Assumption and Omitted Variables**

    Of course, a simple model isn't always perfectly applicable to complex practical problems because you cannot just use one simple variable to explain all the variation of another. But it is an excellent starting point because the algebra is very simple. When you use the simple linear regression, we are here to build a solid theoretical idea, and when we move to the next chapter on multiple regression, it is just a simple extension, meaning all these core ideas still hold true. Let's look at an example regarding housing prices that we talked about before. Last week, I highlighted the relationship between housing prices and the number of years of lease remaining. In Singapore, HDB public housing operates on a fixed lease. For a brand new apartment, it is good for 99 years. Over on the Y-axis, you have the price per square meter. As time goes by, the lease years naturally go in a descending direction—beginning at 99 years, then they become 98, then 97, and so forth. If you think about moving in the opposite direction, the longer the lease remaining, the higher the housing price should be. We can draw a fitted line through this data, which I will show you how to calculate later. However, in economics, unlike machine learning which just wants to tease out predictive associations, we are fiercely focused on causal interpretations. When we want causal interpretations, it is heavily related to the zero conditional mean assumption. This assumption states that the expected value of the error term U, conditional on the value of X, must be zero: E[u|x] = 0. Notice there is a big difference between saying the unconditional expectation of U is zero, E[u] = 0, and saying the conditional expectation E[u|x] = 0. E[u|x] = 0 means that given X at any specific value, the average of all unobserved factors is zero. Because you see, at x = 1 (one year left), you have a specific distribution of U. At x = 2, you have another distribution of U. For any such distribution, the expectation of U given any X must have a value of zero. That condition is much stronger than just saying the overall average of U is zero, and it is precisely why it is so notoriously hard to arrive at a definitive causal interpretation from observational data. If you try to interpret the coefficient β1—which is the slope of this line—you might say that one additional year of lease remaining is associated with a specific price increase derived from the regression. But realistically, we likely cannot give this a true causal interpretation because the zero conditional mean assumption is highly violated here. I will give you a concrete example. Suppose you are comparing apartments where one has more years of lease remaining, meaning the apartment was built more recently. Over time, construction technology improves, and construction materials also improve. That implies the intrinsic quality of newer apartments might also be higher. Because our simple regression does not control for the quality of the building materials or the paint, that quality factor gets dumped into our error term, U. So, conditional on a higher X value (more lease remaining), the expected value of the unobserved quality is actually changing year by year. That clearly violates the zero conditional mean assumption. A student brings up another point: even if the physical quality does not change, reducing the lease remaining by one year inherently implies the physical age of the apartment is getting one year older. When buyers look at an apartment, do they prefer brand new ones or old ones? They usually prefer new ones. So the price change could be due to either the legal lease remaining changing or the physical age of the object changing. That is why the causal relationship is muddied. There are many unobserved factors that can lead to the violation of the zero conditional mean assumption.

    We have covered the theoretical population parameters; now, how can we actually estimate these β parameters from our sample? In our real dataset, when we have a scatter plot of dots, we want to draw a fitted line. That line is the direct result of our estimation procedure. How do we pick that line? It is fundamentally going to depend on how good that line predicts the actual values. That is why they say we choose parameters so that the model fits the data as good as possible. But what does "as good as possible" mathematically mean? How do you define "good"? Before we define "good," let's first look at the predicted value. The predicted value equation is very clear: given the estimated parameters, normally denoted with a "hat" as β0_hat and β1_hat, we generate a prediction. For any given β0_hat and β1_hat, we can establish a sample regression function: y_hat = β0_hat + β1_hat * x. If you have the predicted value (y_hat), you can calculate the residual, or the prediction error itself. This is not the actual unobserved population error 'u'; it is the residual based on your specific prediction line, denoted as u_hat. So that prediction error u_hat equals the actual realization y_i minus the predicted y_i_hat. Now we are finally ready to define what constitutes a "good" fit. A good fit in OLS means we choose parameters that minimize the sum of the squared residual terms. So, the parameters that minimize this sum of squares are called our Ordinary Least Squares estimators. A student is asking, why squared? This feels a bit arbitrary, right? Have you ever thought about why we don't just want to minimize the sum of u_hat itself? What would happen if we just summed the plain residuals? Because positive errors (dots above the line) and negative errors (dots below the line) would simply cancel each other out. Clearly, you don't want a wildly inaccurate line to look perfect just because the massive positive and negative errors sum to zero. So what about absolute values, or power of three, or power of four? Minimizing absolute values is valid and is used in some forms of econometrics, but the mathematical properties for squared terms are vastly easier to derive because the function is smooth and continuously differentiable everywhere. That is why almost everyone historically focused on this specific criterion: minimizing the sum of squared residuals. Imagine we have a scatterplot of many points. If we pick one random line, we calculate the u_hat for this point, for that point, square them, and sum them. But remember, there could be infinite different lines drawn through that cloud of dots, each providing a different sum of squares. What OLS does is mathematically guarantee that we pick the one absolute optimal line that minimizes that sum. There is no other linear estimator that can exist with a smaller sum of squared residuals for that sample. To derive the formula, you simply take the partial derivatives of the summation function with respect to β0_hat and β1_hat. Why do we take the partial derivatives with respect to β0_hat and β1_hat and not X or Y? Because those β estimates are the control levers we are trying to vary to find the minimum. You vary β0 and β1 to minimize the sum of squares. When you do mathematical minimization, you take the derivative with respect to whatever variable you want to optimize and set it equal to zero. You end up with two equations, which we call the normal equations, and you can solve them algebraically. The ultimate solution reveals that β1_hat is equal to the sample covariance of X and Y divided by the sample variance of X. Specifically, the sum of (x_i - x_bar)*(y_i - y_bar) divided by the sum of (x_i - x_bar)^2. Once you have β1_hat, β0_hat is very easy to calculate. β0_hat simply equals y_bar minus β1_hat times x_bar. From this mathematical reality, it is already implied that the sum of the OLS residuals, u_hat, must inherently equal zero. Both sides balance out through the mean point (x_bar, y_bar). That firmly implies that the mean of your predicted y_hat will perfectly equal the sample mean of your actual Y. So whenever you run any linear regression using OLS estimators, the line will always flawlessly fit through the central mean of your sample.

    Next, we are talking about how to measure the overall fit of your regression. We should focus on the standard metrics. We need a holistic measure of the variation of your Y variable. Think about all the total variation present in your sample data, and then think about the variation explained by your estimation. Finally, consider the variation of the residual errors left over from your estimation. Here we rely on the sums of squared terms, and structurally, they naturally have to be mathematically positive. SST is the Total Sum of Squares, which measures the total variation in Y. SSE is the Explained Sum of Squares, and SSR is the Residual Sum of Squares. Basically, the decomposition SST = SSE + SSR comes from our core mathematical properties. During our review, we proved that the variance of (X + Y) equals the variance of X plus the variance of Y, assuming X and Y are completely independent. When we run the OLS regression, we have already mechanically ensured that the residuals u_hat are completely independent of our independent variable X. If they are completely uncorrelated by algebraic design, they have that linear decomposition property. Remember, y is structurally composed of your predicted y_hat plus your residual u_hat. Because y_hat is just a linear function of X, and X is uncorrelated with u_hat, it follows that y_hat and u_hat are independent. So the total variance in Y splits neatly into the variance of y_hat and the variance of u_hat. That is fundamentally why SST always perfectly equals the sum of SSE plus SSR. Now think about how we define "good." If your X variable can explain a massive amount of the variation in Y, that inherently means your residual errors are very small. You naturally want to have a very small SSR. The smaller the SSR is, the better your estimated model fits the data. A small SSR dictates a larger SSE, because they must sum together to your fixed SST. For different models competing on the same dataset—because your data dictates a fixed SST—a better regression model implies a functionally smaller SSR and a correspondingly larger SSE. So naturally, what we do to standardize this is we define a metric called R-squared. R-squared is equal to SSE divided by SST. Equivalently, it is equal to 1 minus (SSR divided by SST). You can confidently say that the higher the R-squared, the better the fit of the model to the specific sample. However, please carefully note my phrasing: it means a better *fit* of the model, not necessarily a universally *better causal model*. For example, running a linear regression using our housing data in statistical software gives us an output table separating the model part and the residual part.     Now we start to talk about the deeper statistical properties, specifically the expected value and variance of the OLS estimators. First, I need to make sure everyone understands what an estimator is. A specific mathematical formula used to estimate a parameter is called an estimator. Once you plug sample data into that formula, you come up with a specific numerical estimate. Your estimate is just a fixed number. But the OLS estimator formula mathematically behaves as a random variable. Why? Because it is fundamentally a mathematical function constructed from two sample variables, X and Y. Because the underlying data X and Y drawn from the population are random variables, your resulting estimator driven by them will inherently also be a random variable. Look at the formula for β0_hat = y_bar - β1_hat * x_bar. For any given random sample you draw, you will pull one specific set of names or entities. In another sample, you will pull a completely different set. The statistical estimates you calculate will absolutely vary across those different samples. Let me use an analogy: if I want to estimate the mean body weight of this class, and I take a sample today, I get one mean. If I take a completely different sample of students next week as people appear and disappear, the body weight mean will shift. Since the resulting estimate always fluctuates depending on the sample drawn, it possesses a probability distribution. Given that it is a distribution, what are the most critical questions we have? We care intensely about its center (the mean) and its spread (the variance). For the mean side, what defines a "good" property? Ideally, we desperately want the expected mean value of our estimator across infinite samples to be exactly the same as the true unobserved population parameter. If, on average, the estimates you generate stubbornly differ from the actual true parameter, you have a terrible, biased estimator.

    To mathematically guarantee this unbiasedness property, econometrics relies on four foundational assumptions. The first one is straightforward: the model must be linear in parameters. Remember, we just modeled log-level and log-log equations—those are completely fine because the variables X and Y are transformed, but the beta parameters themselves remain perfectly linear. The mathematical parameter must be linear; that is the core definition of linear regression. Second, we require a true random sample. We desperately want a randomized draw from the population. We do not want highly selective, biased samples. If your drawn sample mechanically does not represent the entire population, how can you validly derive a causal relationship that applies universally to the population? The third assumption focuses exclusively on the independent variable: there must be sample variation in X, meaning the variance of X must be strictly larger than zero. It obviously cannot be negative, but if it equals zero, it means X is just a constant number. If every single person in this classroom possesses the exact same years of education, how can you possibly isolate and mathematically calculate how varying years of education affect wages? You have absolutely no variation to study. So, if you do not have any variance in your X variables, the denominator in our slope formula hits zero, and you cannot mathematically derive anything. Finally, the fourth and most aggressively important assumption is the zero conditional mean assumption: E[U|X] = 0. This is the crucial linchpin when we try to derive a genuine causal relationship. Returning to the housing regression, this implies that the baseline average of all unobserved factors affecting housing prices must be equal at any specific level of lease remaining. A student might ask, how can the unobserved constant be zero if things like "baseline quality" exist? Rather than getting confused by thinking "how can quality realistically equal zero," understand this mathematically as a simple normalization trick. Any constant average effect is mathematically absorbed straight into the intercept term, β0. Once the intercept absorbs that constant baseline, what remains inside the error U is cleanly normalized around a mean of zero. It is essentially a normalization process so that the fluctuating error isn't continuously biasing our slope parameter.

    (现在我们开始讨论更深层的统计性质，特别是OLS估计量的期望值和方差。首先，我需要确保每个人都明白什么是估计量（estimator）。用于估计某个参数的特定数学公式被称为估计量。一旦你把样本数据代入那个公式，你就会得出一个特定的数值估计（estimate）。你的估计值只是一个固定的数字。但是，OLS估计量公式在数学表现上就像一个随机变量。为什么？因为它从根本上是一个由两个样本变量X和Y构建的数学函数。由于从总体中抽取的底层数据X和Y是随机变量，由它们驱动的生成的估计量，本质上也将是一个随机变量。看看公式 β0_hat = y_bar - β1_hat * x_bar。对于你抽取的任何一个给定随机样本，你会抽出一组特定的名字或实体。在另一个样本中，你将抽出完全不同的一组。你计算出的统计估计值绝对会在这些不同的样本之间产生波动。让我打个比方：如果我想估计这个班级的平均体重，我今天抽取一个样本，得到一个均值。如果我下周随着人们的出现和离开，抽取一个完全不同的学生样本，体重均值就会改变。由于得出的估计值总是随着抽取的样本而波动，所以它具有概率分布。既然它是一个分布，我们最关键的问题是什么？我们极度关注它的中心（均值）和它的分布广度（方差）。对于均值方面，什么定义了“好”的性质？理想情况下，我们极其希望我们的估计量在无限样本中的预期均值与真实的、未观察到的总体参数完全相同。如果平均而言，你生成的估计值顽固地偏离了实际的真实参数，那你就有一个糟糕的、有偏的估计量。

    为了在数学上保证这种无偏性（unbiasedness），计量经济学依赖于四个基本假设。第一个很直接：模型必须在参数上是线性的。记住，我们刚刚对对数-水平和对数-对数方程进行了建模——这些完全没问题，因为虽然变量X和Y被转换了，但Beta参数本身仍然是完全线性的。数学参数必须是线性的；这是线性回归的核心定义。第二，我们需要真实的随机抽样。我们极度渴望从总体中进行随机抽取。我们不希望有高度选择性的、有偏的样本。如果你的抽取样本在机制上不能代表整个总体，你怎么能有效地推导出一个普遍适用于总体的因果关系呢？第三个假设完全集中在自变量上：X必须有样本变异，这意味着X的方差必须严格大于零。它显然不能是负的，但如果它等于零，这意味着X只是一个常数。如果这个教室里的每一个人都有完全一样的受教育年限，你怎么可能去隔离并在数学上计算不同受教育年限如何影响工资呢？你绝对没有变异可以研究。所以，如果你的X变量没有任何方差，我们斜率公式中的分母就会变成零，你在数学上什么也推导不出来。最后，第四个也是极其重要最关键的假设是零条件均值假设：E[U|X] = 0。这是我们试图推导真正因果关系时的关键枢纽。回到住房回归，这意味着所有影响房价的未观察因素的基线平均值，在任何特定的剩余租期水平上都必须相等。可能会有学生问，如果像“基线质量”这样的东西存在，未观察到的常数怎么可能为零呢？与其因为思考“质量怎么可能现实中等于零”而感到困惑，不如在数学上将其理解为一个简单的标准化（normalization）技巧。任何恒定的平均效应都会在数学上被直接吸收到截距项 β0 中。一旦截距吸收了那个恒定的基线，误差 U 里面剩下的东西就被干净地标准化为均值为零。这本质上是一个标准化过程，这样波动的误差就不会持续使我们的斜率参数产生偏差。)

  - [19:47:06 - 19:58:52] **Variance of Estimators and Homoscedasticity**

    Once we satisfy those four strict assumptions, we can formally guarantee the property of unbiasedness. Unbiasedness comes strictly from the mathematical proof that the expected value of your estimator uniformly equals the true population parameter across infinite samples. Notice I repeatedly emphasize "on average." Unfortunately, anytime you run a real regression in life, you are trapped working with only one specific given sample realization. So even though the estimator is perfectly unbiased on average across infinite potential samples, for your specific data reality, your isolated estimate can be uncomfortably far away from the true population X parameter. Simply put, unbiasedness does not magically guarantee that the single number you get is the exact true parameter. To evaluate how far away we reasonably expect our estimate to drift, we absolutely must analyze the variance of the estimator. What do we want regarding variance? Likely, the critically smaller the variance is, the better the quality of the estimate. Think of evaluating body weights again. If this week the average estimate is 55 kilograms, and next week a wild sample swing gives me 100 kilograms, the estimate quality is frankly terrible. But if today it is 55.0, and next week it is 55.1 or 55.2, the variance is minuscule. The estimate is exceptionally precise and tight. I can confidently tell someone my estimate is extremely accurate. So ultimately, the gold standard is an estimator with both an unbiased expected value and the smallest possible variance. The variance of the OLS estimator inherently depends heavily on the internal variation of your unobserved error term, U. If the true population error U swings wildly, your resulting estimate will also swing wildly. However, it is not just the overall variance of U that matters. We strongly desire a property called homoscedasticity. Homoscedasticity demands that the variance of the unobserved error term U must remain completely constant and independent of the value of X. We explicitly do not want a scenario where people with 16 years of education have wildly massive variance in their earnings, while people with only 12 years have a highly compressed, smaller variance.

    ![](https://encrypted-tbn1.gstatic.com/licensed-image?q=tbn:ANd9GcTWg2NW8RDt2SdjasuLGFSXnUhDDsKN3EMY8lg-kCJwt4YQZzmHYJYFfmzT-bqN6gyiBJkblUnn4PuKY64)

    Let me draw a quick diagram to make this crystal clear. Picture a classic regression line for earnings based on education. At 8 years of education, the expected mean earning is located exactly on the regression line. At 14 years, the expected mean earning is higher up on the line. Around each of those points on the line, there is an actual vertical distribution representing the real spread of people's earnings. The strict assumption of homoscedasticity mandates that the exact spread and shape of this distribution must be perfectly identical at 8 years, 12 years, and 14 years. But if you actually observe real labor market data, you see severe heteroscedasticity. The distribution fundamentally alters shape. For individuals with only primary school education, their monthly earnings are tightly clustered, perhaps ranging narrowly from one thousand to three thousand dollars. It is a highly compressed spread. But if you examine individuals averaging ten thousand dollars in salary (highly educated), the realistic range of their peers easily swings from nine thousand to nineteen thousand dollars or more. It exhibits a radically flatter, much wider distribution spread. That reality means the variance is highly dependent on the X variables, explicitly violating the homoscedasticity assumption. Finally, assuming we possess perfect homoscedasticity, the variance formula for our slope estimator, Var(β1_hat), simplifies beautifully. It is equal to the constant variance of the error term (sigma squared) divided by the total sum of squares of the independent variable X. The intuition here is immensely powerful: a larger, noisier error term drastically blows up the variance of your estimate. Conversely, having massive variation spread out across your independent X variable is incredibly helpful. If your X data points are heavily concentrated in a tiny cluster, you have barely any horizontal leverage, meaning you can pivot almost any random line through them. But if your X data is widely spread horizontally across the axis, it forcibly locks the slope into place. A massive variation in your X variables drastically increases the precision and efficiency of your estimate by crushing the variance. Okay, that wraps up the theoretical core.     (一旦我们满足了那四个严格的假设，我们就可以正式保证无偏性这个性质。无偏性严格来源于数学证明：即你的估计量在无限样本中的期望值一致等于真实的总体参数。注意我反复强调“平均而言”。不幸的是，在现实生活中，每当你运行真实的回归时，你都被困在只能使用一个特定的给定样本实现上。因此，即使估计量在无限潜在样本中的平均值是完美无偏的，对于你具体的数据现实来说，你孤立的估计值也可能令人不安地偏离真实的总体参数。简单来说，无偏性并不能神奇地保证你得到的那个单一数字就是确切的真实参数。为了评估我们合理预期我们的估计值会偏离多远，我们绝对必须分析估计量的方差。对于方差，我们想要什么？显然，方差越小越关键，估计的质量就越好。再想象一下评估体重。如果本周的平均估计是55公斤，而下周因为样本的大幅波动变成了100公斤，坦率地说，估计质量糟透了。但如果今天是55.0，下周是55.1或55.2，方差就极小。该估计极其精确紧凑。我可以自信地告诉别人我的估计非常准确。所以归根结底，黄金标准是一个既具有无偏期望值，又具有尽可能小方差的估计量。OLS估计量的方差本质上严重依赖于你未观察到的误差项U的内在变异。如果真实的总体误差U剧烈波动，你得出的估计值也会剧烈波动。然而，重要的不仅仅是U的整体方差。我们强烈渴望一种叫做同方差性（Homoscedasticity）的属性。同方差性要求未观察到的误差项U的方差必须保持完全恒定，且独立于X的值。我们明确不希望出现这样一种情况：受过16年教育的人的收入方差极其巨大，而只有12年教育的人的方差却高度压缩、比较小。

    让我快速画个图，把这个讲得一清二楚。想象一条经典的基于教育的收入回归线。在受教育8年时，预期的平均收入正好在回归线上。在14年时，预期的平均收入在线上更高位置。在回归线上这些点的周围，有一个实际的垂直分布，代表了人们实际收入的分散程度。同方差性的严格假设强制要求：在8年、12年和14年时，这种分布的确切跨度和形状必须完美一致。但如果你实际观察真实的劳动力市场数据，你会看到严重的异方差性（Heteroscedasticity）。分布在根本上改变了形状。对于只有小学教育的个体来说，他们的月收入紧密聚集，大概在狭窄的一千到三千美元之间波动。这是一个高度压缩的分散度。但如果你考察平均薪水一万美元的个体（受过高等教育），他们同龄人的现实薪水范围轻易就能从九千波动到一万九千美元或更多。它表现出一种从根本上更平坦、更宽广的分布跨度。这种现实意味着方差高度依赖于X变量，明确违反了同方差性假设。最后，假设我们拥有完美的同方差性，我们斜率估计量的方差公式 Var(β1_hat) 就能被漂亮地简化。它等于误差项的恒定方差（sigma的平方）除以自变量X的总平方和。这里的直觉极其强大：更大、噪音更多的误差项会急剧放大你估计值的方差。相反，在你的自变量X上有巨大的分布变异则非常有帮助。如果你的X数据点严重集中在一个微小的簇中，你几乎没有任何水平杠杆，这意味着你几乎可以用任何随机的线穿过它们。但是如果你的X数据在坐标轴上广泛水平分布，它就会强行把斜率锁定在合适的位置。你X变量的巨大变异通过压低方差，极大地提高了你估计的精确度和有效性。好了，这就结束了理论核心部分。现在是我们短暂休息的时间了。现在差不多8点了；我们8点10分回来。)
