# ___NMC5363 AI, Media, and Social Transformations___
***

## Course syllabus
- **Course Description** This interdisciplinary master’s course interrogates artificial intelligence not merely as technology but as an institutionalised medium that channels data, resources, and power, reshaping everyday life. Drawing on media and communications studies, sociology, political economy, critical AI studies, and Science and Technology Studies, it explores: (1) the historical and geographical trajectories of AI development; (2) AI’s integration into work, labour, creativity and social norms; and (3) legal-ethical debates on intellectual property, privacy, sustainability, disinformation and algorithmic bias across race, gender and class. Through critical readings, case studies and a research project, students analyse AI’s local and global impacts and debate future directions. The course is non-technical (no coding required), aimed at students seeking nuanced insight into AI’s role in reorganising economy, culture and governance.
- **Learning outcomes**
  - Students will develop a contextual understanding of AI, its history, and evolution, to have an informed opinion for its future use and trajectory.
  - Students will understand the basics of the technologies behind AI, such as machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing, neural networks, and algorithms.
  - Students will be able to critically analyse AI as a form of media that structures and mediates data, information, social interactions, and work practices, and understand its broader societal implications.
  - Students will be able to examine the impact of AI on media and cultural industries, such as journalism, advertising, entertainment, and cultural production, considering AI-driven automation and personalisation.
  - Students will be able to evaluate the ethical, social, and regulatory challenges surrounding AI, such as algorithmic bias, disinformation, surveillance, intellectual property rights, and data governance.

# Week 1
## Week 1 Summary
- **The Definition and Scope of AI 人工智能的定义与范畴**
  - **UNESCO Framework:** Defines AI as an integration of models and algorithms based on information processing technologies to perform cognitive tasks, prediction, and decision-making.
  - **Umbrella Term:** AI is not a singular, monolithic system, but rather a catch-all category for heterogeneous data processing technologies mimicking human intelligence.
  - **教科文组织框架：** 将AI定义为基于信息处理技术的模型和算法的整合，用于执行认知任务、预测和决策。
  - **统称术语：** AI并不是一个单一的、铁板一块的系统，而是模仿人类智能的异构数据处理技术的统称。
- **Sociotechnical Imaginaries 社会技术想象**
  - **Core Concept:** A collectively held, institutionally stabilized vision of desirable futures animated by science and technology (Jasanoff & Kim).
  - **Structural Power:** Narratives and sci-fi tropes (utopian or dystopian) are not just stories; they directly dictate public policy, research funding, and physical infrastructure (e.g., US vs. South Korea nuclear policies).
  - **核心概念：** 由科学技术激发的一种集体持有的、制度上稳定的关于理想未来的愿景（贾萨诺夫与金）。
  - **结构性力量：** 叙事和科幻设定（乌托邦或反乌托邦）不仅仅是故事；它们直接决定了公共政策、研究资金和物理基础设施（例如美国与韩国的核政策对比）。
- **The Economics of AI Hype AI炒作的经济学**
  - **AI Winters:** Historical cycles (1970s, 1980s) where AI development stagnated and funding collapsed due to unmet hyper-optimistic promises.
  - **Financial Incentives:** The categorical label of "AI" is used as a powerful marketing strategy to attract venture capital and monopolize global research, forcing non-AI industries to "AI-wash" standard technologies.
  - **AI寒冬：** 历史上的周期（20世纪70年代、80年代），由于未能兑现极度乐观的承诺，AI发展停滞，资金链断裂。
  - **财务激励：** “AI”这一类别标签被用作吸引风险投资和垄断全球研究的强大营销策略，迫使非AI行业对标准技术进行“AI洗绿”。
- **AI Snake Oil and Algorithmic Harm AI万金油与算法伤害**
  - **Predictive Failures:** Statistical models are often sold as magical panaceas but frequently fail in messy human contexts, aggressively amplifying historical biases (e.g., UK A-level grades, Dutch tax scandal, Amazon resume screening).
  - **Accountability Shield:** Treating AI as an autonomous agent allows executives and institutions to deflect blame onto "neutral algorithms," evading responsibility for structural flaws.
  - **Ghost Work:** The illusion of autonomous magic obscures the massive, underpaid human workforce required to label data, moderate content, and train models.
  - **预测失败：** 统计模型经常被当作神奇的万能药来兜售，但在复杂的现实人类环境中却屡屡失败，并严重放大历史偏见（例如英国A-level成绩、荷兰税务丑闻、亚马逊简历筛选）。
  - **问责盾牌：** 将AI视为自主代理使得高管和机构能够将责任推卸给“中立的算法”，从而逃避对结构性缺陷的责任。
  - **幽灵工作：** 自主魔法的错觉掩盖了标记数据、审核内容和训练模型所需的大量廉价人类劳动力。
- **The Control Revolution and Infrastructure 控制革命与基础设施**
  - **Infrastructural Shift:** Like steam power and railways, AI is an infrastructure fundamentally reshaping the architecture of the state, law, and human communication to manage the chaos of technological upheaval.
  - **Political Project:** AI is not a perfectly objective mathematical method, but a highly aspirational, data-centric political project designed to exert power and extract value.
  - **基础设施转型：** 就像蒸汽动力和铁路一样，AI是一种基础设施，从根本上重塑了国家、法律和人类交流的架构，以管理技术剧变带来的混乱。
  - **政治项目：** AI并不是一种完美客观的数学方法，而是一个高度雄心勃勃的、以数据为中心的政治项目，旨在行使权力和榨取价值。
## Week 1 Learning Tips
Welcome to the course! As we transition into this material, I want you to step back from treating AI purely as a series of math equations, statistical weights, or coding workflows. While technical mechanics matter, you must start building an intuition for the "sociological architecture" of AI. When you read about a new AI capability in the news, do not just ask "How accurate is the model?" Ask "Who funded this? Whose historical data trained it? Who bears the cost of its failure?" This shift in perspective from the technical to the socio-political is the key to mastering this course.

欢迎来到这门课程！当我们在学习这些材料时，我希望你们退后一步，不要仅仅把AI看作是一系列的数学方程式、统计权重或编程工作流。虽然技术机制很重要，但你们必须开始建立对AI“社会学架构”的直觉。当你在新闻中读到一项新的AI功能时，不要只问“这个模型有多准确？”而是要问“谁资助了它？它是由谁的历史数据训练出来的？谁来承担它失败的代价？”这种从技术向社会政治视角的转变是掌握这门课程的关键。

A common conceptual pitfall for students studying technology is to dismiss hype and science fiction narratives as mere marketing fluff or abstract philosophy. Do not make this mistake. You need to deeply understand the "why" behind sociotechnical imaginaries. These visions are structural mechanisms that secure billions in funding and literally write national policies. As you review this week's material, practice mapping out the direct connection between a utopian claim (like AI solving global climate change) and the structural financial incentives of the mega-corporations making that claim.

学习技术的学生常犯的一个概念性错误是，把炒作和科幻叙事仅仅当作营销噱头或抽象的哲学而置之不理。千万别犯这个错误。你需要深刻理解社会技术想象背后的“原因”。这些愿景是确保获得数十亿资金和切实制定国家政策的结构性机制。在复习本周的材料时，请练习理清一种乌托邦式主张（比如AI解决全球气候变化）与提出该主张的巨头企业的结构性财务动机之间的直接联系。

When we discuss AI governance and algorithmic harm, avoid treating ethical guidelines as merely a bureaucratic compliance checklist that engineers must tick off. True governance is deeply messy and rooted in human realities. To study effectively for this module, anchor the theoretical concepts of "AI Snake Oil" to the tangible case studies we discussed in the lecture, such as the UK A-level algorithm or the Dutch tax scandal. Memorize the mechanisms of how these failures occurred—not as coding bugs, but as structural deployments of biased historical assumptions—so you can construct strong, evidence-based arguments in your upcoming seminar debates.

- [12:53 - 12:56] **Failures in Predictive AI Systems**

  One catastrophic example was during the Coronavirus pandemic in the UK. Because students couldn't take their physical A-level exams, an algorithm was used to predict their final results based on their past performance and the historical performance of their schools. Now, at the time, the government genuinely thought it was a good idea, as taking physical tests was not an option. But it resulted in a fiasco where students from historically poorer schools were systematically downgraded, while those from elite schools were artificially boosted. The algorithm graded their postcode, not their ability. Another severe example happened recently, between 2019 and 2020, with the Dutch government. They faced collapse over a child benefits scandal. The Dutch tax authority used a predictive algorithm that wrongly claimed tens of thousands of families were claiming fraudulent childcare benefits. They pursued these parents, pushing them into financial ruin, forcing them to pay back money they legitimately deserved. A year later, a parliamentary inquiry found that these accusations were completely flawed for many reasons. To simply call it a "claim of damage" is too weak; people were pushed into financial crises, severe psychological distress, and families were torn apart. These are not small glitches. Why does this happen? We all know Generative AI like ChatGPT produces hallucinations, spitting out wrong information even if you explicitly tell it your policy. Whenever you ask it, "Why did you do that?", it just says, "Oh, I did it, I'm sorry." But this flaw applies to both generative AI and predictive AI. There are many reasons why these predictions fail, largely due to embedded biases.

- [13:19 - 13:29] **The Six Reasons for the Claims-Reality Gap**

  There are many possible answers to why this gap persists, including the excellent points you've raised. I jotted down a few structural reasons based on Narayanan and Kapoor's arguments. First: incentives reward overclaiming. We are living in a capitalist world, and money is often the driving factor. Vendors and start-ups need attention, funding, and rapid adoption, so marketing presents the AI as a general-purpose tool that is completely ready for deployment. Imagine advertising an AI that only does one very specific, narrow task. You would raise much less money compared to pitching a General Purpose Technology. Second: AI capability is easy to demo, but very hard to evaluate in context. This is a massive reason why the gap isn't just because people are stupid or gullible. People see a polished demo and believe it. But a system can score well on a test yet be totally unreliable, unsafe, or unhelpful in everyday use. An AI is a sociotechnical system. To use the criminal justice algorithm as an example: developers building the AI software don't have access to the messy, real-world data sets that local police use daily. They test it in controlled settings. When they deploy it, the AI's performance degrades because we don't know how human workers flexibly or innovatively use (or misuse) these technologies. Third: ambiguous problem definitions, which I call "goalpost shifting." When an AI fails to deliver, the creators just change the definition of what success looks like. Fourth: reality shifts and data lag. Models learn from past data, but the world changes. A serious issue happened between 2022 and 2023 when the Russia-Ukraine war disrupted global data patterns, causing predictive models trained on peaceful periods to fail spectacularly. Fifth: hidden labor and infrastructure. Think of Siri, Alexa, or social media content moderation. We often completely forget about the human labor involved. Specifically, the cheap labor in countries across Africa and Southeast Asia. There are thousands of people working in sweatshop conditions to annotate, label, and classify data. They are the ones tagging an image as ethical or violent so the AI can learn. We don't see this hidden human foundation; we just see the "magic" of the AI. Sixth: accountability is often weak. When systems fail, responsibility is diffuse. The vendor blames the deployer, the deployer blames the frontline user, and harms are treated as unavoidable "errors." Based on all of this, my conceptualization of AI is that it is not a technically precise label. AI is an aspirational, sometimes fantastical project that encompasses an expanding collection of data-centric technologies. We are not tearing it down because we hate it; we are deconstructing it because we want to understand what AI really means. What it can do, what it cannot do, and what it *should* not do. We need to analyze it not just as a technology, but socio-culturally, politically, and economically. We want to move beyond the binary thinking of "is AI good or bad?" to be more specific and contextual about its implementation. Alright, it is 1:30 PM.   为什么这种差距会持续存在，有很多可能的答案，包括你们提出的精彩观点。我根据 Narayanan 和 Kapoor 的论点记下了几个结构性原因。第一：激励机制奖励夸大其词。我们生活在一个资本主义世界，金钱往往是驱动因素。供应商和初创公司需要关注、资金和快速采用，因此营销将人工智能包装成一种完全可以部署的通用工具。想象一下，如果你宣传的人工智能只能完成一项非常具体、狭窄的任务。与推销通用技术（General Purpose Technology）相比，你筹集的资金要少得多。第二：人工智能的能力很容易演示，但在实际环境中却很难评估。这也是为什么出现这种差距的原因不仅仅是因为人们愚蠢或轻信。人们看到一个精美的演示就相信了它。但一个系统可能在测试中得分很高，但在日常使用中却完全不可靠、不安全或毫无帮助。人工智能是一个社会技术系统。以刑事司法算法为例：构建人工智能软件的开发人员无法访问当地警察每天使用的那些混乱的、真实世界的数据集。他们在受控环境中进行测试。当他们部署它时，人工智能的性能会下降，因为我们不知道人类工作者是如何灵活或创新地使用（或滥用）这些技术的。第三：模糊的问题定义，我称之为“移动球门（goalpost shifting）”。当人工智能未能达到预期效果时，创造者只需改变成功的定义。第四：现实世界的变化和数据滞后。模型从过去的数据中学习，但世界在变。2022年至2023年间发生了一个严重的问题，当时俄乌战争扰乱了全球数据模式，导致在和平时期训练的预测模型惨败。第五：隐藏的劳动力和基础设施。想想 Siri、Alexa 或社交媒体的内容审核。我们常常完全忘记了其中涉及的人类劳动力。具体来说，是非洲和东南亚国家廉价的劳动力。有成千上万的人在血汗工厂般的条件下工作，对数据进行注释、标记和分类。正是他们将图像标记为合乎伦理的或暴力的，以便人工智能进行学习。我们看不到这种隐藏的人类基础；我们只看到了人工智能的“魔法”。第六：问责制往往很薄弱。当系统发生故障时，责任是分散的。供应商指责部署者，部署者指责一线用户，而造成的伤害被视为不可避免的“错误”。基于所有这些，我对人工智能的概念化理解是，它不是一个技术上精确的标签。人工智能是一个充满抱负的、有时甚至是不切实际的项目，它包含了一系列不断扩展的以数据为中心的技术。我们剖析它不是因为我们讨厌它；我们解构它是因为我们想了解人工智能的真正含义。它能做什么，不能做什么，以及它“不应该”做什么。我们不仅需要将它作为一种技术来分析，还需要从社会文化、政治和经济的角度来分析。我们希望超越“人工智能是好还是坏？”这种二元对立的思维，而是针对其实施情况进行更具体、更结合语境的探讨。好了，现在是下午1:30。我想现在是快速休息一下的好时间。我们休息大约10分钟，1:40回来继续研讨会的剩余部分。

- [13:43 - 13:49] **AI as Media and Social Transformation**

  Welcome back. Before the break, we were discussing the functional aspects of the change in communication. Now, people are seeing how AI may be fundamentally affecting the way we interact and communicate. If communication has historically been defined by what it means to be human, what happens when machines move in to communicate? What is the difference between machine communication and human communication? This is how we can think about AI as a medium. AI isn't just a tool we communicate *through*; it is an entity we communicate *with*. This shifts the theoretical foundation from just Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) to Human-Machine Communication (HMC). But the reason our course title includes "Social Transformations" is because communication is about much more than what happens simply between individuals. It immediately raises a much bigger macro-level question: why do humans, institutions, and organizations communicate in the first place? Yes, we want to express ourselves, and we want to connect with one another socially, emotionally, and politically. But at the same time, communication is how society coordinates itself. It is how institutions make decisions. It is how rules are enforced, how societal value is assigned, how uncertainty is managed, and how legitimacy is produced—for example, through the media or political speeches. Communication is the mechanism for societal coordination, not just individual heuristic exchange. So, when AI steps into the role of communicator, it reshapes communication in ways that have stakes which are not just individual or interpersonal, but deeply institutional and structural.

  There is actually a great, classic book on this. It's a bit older and has nothing explicitly to do with AI, which is why I didn't put it on the reading list, but I highly recommend it. It is called *The Control Revolution*, written in 1986 by the American sociologist James Beniger. He analyzed media, communication, and information technologies as systems built fundamentally to maintain coordination and control as societies scaled up and became vastly more complex. For example, he looked at the 18th and 19th-century scientific revolutions—steam power and the railways. He argued that while these innovations were amazing, they caused a profound "crisis of control." Societies had to develop new means to address this crisis to continue coordinating. It wasn't just about the government oppressively controlling people; it was about how a rapidly growing society manages logistics. To handle the speed of railways and industrial production, societies developed the telegraph, bureaucratic management, and standardized time zones just to maintain control of information flows across complex networks. If you are interested in how technological innovation mandates the measurement and control of information, I highly recommend this book. If we think about AI from this perspective, why is AI being developed? We will discuss this in Week 3 when we cover political economy. Some people argue we are developing AI to reach AGI—the ultimate dream for people like Ray Kurzweil. But at the same time, AI is simply another mechanism for coordinating and controlling society. It is a new way of managing massive scales of data, restructuring how society is organized. When a communication technology this powerful is introduced, it will obviously have massive macro-level societal implications. This is why we call this course "AI, Media, and Social Transformations." We aren't just talking about AI in the media industry; we are looking at AI as a medium of communication that drives huge socio-economic changes, just like the telegraph and modern bureaucracy did in the past.

    You might be wondering, why do we actually have to spend so much time analyzing these narratives? These are just imaginations, visions, and science fiction stories, right? This is a society-wide vision that goes far beyond mere speculation or futuristic forecasting. These narratives actively dictate public policy and fundamentally shape not only how a technology is perceived by the public, but how it is financially backed, developed, and physically implemented across the world. The seminal definition of a sociotechnical imaginary comes from Sheila Jasanoff and Sang-Hyun Kim in their highly influential 2015 book, *Dreamscapes of Modernity*. They define it as "a collectively held, institutionally stabilized, and publicly performing vision of desirable futures, animated by shared understandings of forms of social life and social order, attainable through and supported by advances in science and technology." They initially developed this framework in a landmark 2009 article comparing the sociotechnical imaginaries surrounding nuclear power in South Korea versus the United States. They brilliantly demonstrated how a mere "vision" can physically alter a technology's trajectory. In the United States, nuclear power was fundamentally viewed through the lens of the atomic bombs dropped during World War II. Consequently, the American imaginary surrounding nuclear energy was one of strict containment—a terrifying power that had to be heavily controlled and restrained. This fearful imaginary birthed policies like President Eisenhower's "Atoms for Peace" initiative and the Price-Anderson Nuclear Industries Indemnity Act, alongside a robust anti-nuclear movement completely focused on the inherent dangers of the technology. By contrast, the imaginary in South Korea looked entirely different, even though they were dealing with the exact same atomic physics. Following the devastation of the Korean War in 1953, South Korea was one of the poorest, most resource-deprived nations on Earth. From the very outset, nuclear power was imagined purely as an instrument of national salvation and rapid industrial development. In 1958, a mere five years after the war ended, while the country was still completely destitute, they boldly established the Atomic Energy Act. In 1959, they created the Atomic Energy Research Institute. They had absolutely no domestic infrastructure, no facilities, and no budget to investigate nuclear power at the time. Yet, they poured everything into it because the national narrative dictated that nuclear power was the only way to leapfrog their poverty and catch up to the first-world economies of Western Europe and North America. That overarching narrative completely altered how nuclear technology was governed, funded, and socially integrated in both countries. Today's topic obviously isn't nuclear physics, so I will stop that historical tangent here, but the core point remains: visions and narratives directly dictate policy development and determine the physical reality of how a technology takes root in society.

    另一位学生批判性地指出了炒作背后的金融机制，将当前的气候与另一场即将到来的“AI寒冬”相提并论。这位学生敏锐地观察到，风险投资家已经向AI开发注入了数万亿美元，但企业正迅速意识到，该技术尚未产生最初承诺的巨大效率或财务价值。为了防止泡沫立即破裂和股价暴跌，公司必须拼命维持炒作以安抚投资者。这非常有可能性。这些初创公司必须偿还他们获得的巨额投资。他们极力使用夸张的手法来保持资金的流动。想想Uber——直到非常近的时期之前，Uber其实从来都不是一家盈利的公司。它在十多年的时间里，像一块巨大的海绵一样吸收着投资者的资本，不断地兜售关于自动驾驶车队的未来导向的叙事来吸引新的投资者，完全无视了他们当时缺乏盈利能力的事实。最后，另一位学生指出了在现代经济中注意力的绝对力量，指出无论关于AI的新闻是极其乌托邦的还是末日般的，这种绝对的极端主导了对话，并将聚光灯牢牢锁定在科技公司身上。我回应说，注意力可以说是当今最有价值的货币。即使是负面新闻也能主导信息生态系统。想想前任美国总统唐纳德·特朗普（Donald Trump）。在他任职期间，你不可能有哪怕一天看不到关于他的新闻。不管你是狂热地支持他还是绝对地鄙视他，这都不重要——他完全垄断了全世界的注意力。科技公司利用了完全相同的动态。如果他们能用拯救或毁灭的极端承诺来垄断全球对话，他们就可以赌一把，希望现实最终能赶上炒作。

  - [13:56:25 - 14:05:54] **Deconstructing AI Hype and Structural Constraints**

    What you all articulated in the discussion hits perfectly on the core points. I want to formally synthesize these structural reasons for why the hype persists despite the glaring failures. First, it is fundamentally about financial incentives. Vendors, rapidly growing startups, and massive behemoths like Google and Amazon desperately need your attention, your funding, and institutional adoption. Marketing AI as a super-intelligence effectively tells investors, "We are reaching for the stars, we are building AGI. Continue investing in us, even if you don't see immediate profitability." If you are a small startup, that hyperbole is the only way to stretch your funding. Second, AI capabilities are astonishingly easy to demonstrate in a slick YouTube video, but excruciatingly hard to validate in messy organizational contexts. In the real world, you are dealing with tired humans, immense time pressures, and wildly uncooperative infrastructure. Consider "Excalibur," a highly publicized AI diagnostic tool developed in South Korea for veterinary clinics to detect diseases in dogs and cats from x-rays. It worked flawlessly in the multi-million dollar lab, but failed miserably in rural clinics simply because the local hospitals lacked high-speed internet, or because cheap fluorescent lighting in the examination rooms fundamentally distorted the optical sensors the AI relied upon. It sounds incredibly petty, but basic physical infrastructure dictates how this knowledge exists in reality.

    Third, tech firms use highly ambiguous problem definitions, which allows them to constantly shift the goalposts of success. If a startup sells an emotion-detection AI to "improve the hiring process," what does that actually mean? Does it mean reducing racial bias? Maximizing corporate profit? Increasing employee retention? Because the definition is so vague, they never actually have to prove they definitively solved the problem. Fourth, AI inherently learns from imperfect, historically biased real-world data, which we will deeply explore in week nine. The data does not reflect objective reality; it reflects the deep systemic biases and inequalities of the era in which it was captured. Fifth, the hype completely erases the massive human labor and vast physical infrastructure that actually makes these systems function, creating the illusion of autonomous magic. We call this "ghost work"—the millions of underpaid workers in the global south classifying data, labeling toxic content, and explicitly fine-tuning these models so they appear intelligent. We will cover this heavily in week four. And we cannot forget the physical infrastructure: data centers, water cooling systems, and refineries mining rare earth metals in horrific conditions. AI isn't an ethereal brain floating in the cloud; it is a hyper-connected network of human blood, sweat, and physical extraction. Finally, treating AI as an autonomous agent creates an incredibly convenient shield of weaker accountability. When a mid-level manager at a major corporation like Starbucks or Coca-Cola makes a catastrophic mistake, someone is fired or a CEO is forced to resign. But when an AI algorithm denies thousand of citizens their legal tax benefits, or when a massive platform like X allows users to generate deeply non-consensual deepfake pornography using real images of women, the executives just throw their hands up. They blame the neutral algorithm or the users' prompts, completely dodging the accountability for building the flawed architecture in the first place. Therefore, I want you to carry this specific framing with you: AI is not a technically precise, perfectly objective mathematical method. It is a highly aspirational, deeply political project that deliberately obscures its own messiness. It encompasses an ever-expanding collection of data-centric tools designed to exert power and extract value. The primary objective of this entire course is to ruthlessly deconstruct this political project and understand what AI actually does to our social reality.

    你们在讨论中所表达的内容完美地击中了核心要点。我想把这些结构性原因正式综合起来，解释为什么尽管存在明显的失败，炒作依然持续。首先，这从根本上是关于财务激励。供应商、快速成长的初创公司以及像谷歌和亚马逊这样的庞然大物，极度渴望你们的注意力、资金和机构层面的采用。将AI作为一种超级智能来进行营销，实际上是在告诉投资者：“我们正在摘星，我们正在构建AGI。继续投资我们吧，即使你没有看到立竿见影的盈利。”如果你是一家小型初创公司，这种夸张的宣传是你拉长资金链的唯一方式。其次，AI的功能在光鲜亮丽的YouTube视频中展示起来令人惊叹地容易，但在混乱的组织环境中验证起来却极其困难。在现实世界中，你需要应对疲惫的人类、巨大的时间压力以及极其不配合的基础设施。想想“神剑”（Excalibur），这是韩国开发的一款广受宣传的AI诊断工具，用于在兽医诊所通过X光片检测猫狗的疾病。它在耗资数百万美元的实验室里运行得完美无缺，但在乡村诊所里却败得一塌糊涂，原因仅仅是当地医院缺乏高速互联网，或者检查室里廉价的荧光灯照明从根本上扭曲了AI所依赖的光学传感器。这听起来无比琐碎，但基本的物理基础设施决定了这种知识在现实中是如何存在的。

    第三，科技公司使用了高度模糊的问题定义，这使得他们能够不断地移动代表成功的“球门柱”。如果一家初创公司兜售一款情绪检测AI来“改善招聘流程”，这到底意味着什么？这意味着减少种族偏见吗？实现企业利润最大化？提高员工留存率？因为定义如此模糊，他们实际上从来不需要证明他们明确地解决了问题。第四，AI先天就会从不完美的、存在历史偏见的现实世界数据中学习，我们将在第九周深入探讨这一点。数据并不反映客观现实；它反映了捕获数据那个时代的深层系统性偏见和不平等。第五，这种炒作完全抹去了实际维持这些系统运行的庞大人类劳动和浩大的物理基础设施，制造了自主运作的魔法错觉。我们称之为“幽灵工作”（ghost work）——全球南方数以百万计报酬微薄的工人在对数据进行分类、给有害内容打标签，并对这些模型进行明确的微调，好让它们显得很智能。我们将在第四周重点讨论这个问题。而且我们不能忘记物理基础设施：数据中心、水冷系统以及在恶劣条件下开采稀土金属的精炼厂。AI并不是漂浮在云端的空灵大脑；它是人类血汗与物理榨取构成的高度互联网络。最后，将AI视为一种自主代理，创造了一面极其便利的、弱化问责制的盾牌。当星巴克或可口可乐等大公司的中层管理人员犯下灾难性错误时，有人会被解雇，或者CEO会被迫辞职。但是，当AI算法剥夺了成千上万公民合法的税务福利，或者当像X（Twitter）这样的大型平台允许用户使用女性的真实照片生成深度伪造的、未经同意的色情图片时，高管们只是摊开双手。他们把责任推给所谓中立的算法或用户的提示词，完全逃避了最初构建这个有缺陷架构的责任。因此，我希望你们能牢记这个特定的框架：AI不是一种技术上精确、完美客观的数学方法。它是一个高度雄心勃勃的、具有深刻政治色彩的项目，它故意掩盖了自身的混乱。它包含了一个不断扩大的、以数据为中心的工具集合，旨在行使权力和榨取价值。我们整个课程的首要目标，就是无情地解构这个政治项目，并理解AI实际上对我们的社会现实做了什么。

  - [14:05:54 - 14:18:38] **The Control Revolution and Course Roadmap**

    The future we face will undoubtedly be vastly messier, highly varied, and infinitely more complicated than the pristine utopias promised by big tech billionaires. There is no singular magic algorithm that is going to solve all of our socio-political crises. Our society has been through this exact kind of chaotic technological upheaval before—we saw it with steam power, the railway networks, the telegraph, radio, and television. This is precisely why this course is titled *AI, Media, and Social Transformations*. We are not here to teach you specific prompt engineering for your future jobs. We are here to critically analyze how AI actively restructures how humans and institutions communicate, mediate information, and enforce societal rules. The late, brilliant American historian and sociologist James Beniger wrote a phenomenal book in 1986 called *The Control Revolution*. It is not required reading for this module, but I highly recommend it. He presented a gloomy but vital thesis: when explosive new technologies like the steam engine and the railway violently transformed the physical operations of the economy during the Industrial Revolution, it created a massive crisis of control. Goods were moving faster than human cognition could track them. To manage this terrifying uncertainty, society was forced to frantically invent massive new bureaucracies and information technologies—like the telegraph—just to maintain control and coordinate the flow of physical capital. Today, we are living through a similar crisis of control. AI, much like electricity or the railway, is driving unprecedented societal upheaval. Consequently, the state and civil society must rapidly invent new forms of bureaucratic, legal, and infrastructural governance just to manage the chaos. AI is not just a tool you use individually; it is an infrastructure fundamentally reshaping the entire architecture of the state, the law, and human interaction.

    If we focus entirely on the optimistic, utopian hype propagated by Silicon Valley, we blind ourselves to the actual, gritty reality of how power is being violently redistributed right now on the ground. Therefore, this course investigates AI not from a top-down, theoretical approach, but by analyzing how it is practically deployed by real people responding to raw financial incentives and political interests. To give you a roadmap of how we will tackle this: Next week, we will focus on the technical foundations of AI. We won't delve too deeply into the societal impacts just yet; instead, I will provide a baseline understanding of how these statistical models physically work, heavily grounded in an anthropological rather than purely computer science perspective. In week four, we tackle the Political Economy of AI, dissecting the absolute dominance of the seven massive multinational tech monopolies in the West and the rapidly emerging giants in China. Week five will focus on automation, labor, and work. We will move past simple narratives of job displacement to examine how AI forces a brutal redistribution of invisible labor—focusing on the massive, underpaid human workforce required to label data and correct algorithmic errors on the fly, similar to how human referees heavily mediate the semi-automated offside technology used in the World Cup. Week six will cover how AI alters cultural production and human creativity. Week seven shifts to intimacy, examining human-machine relationships and whether AI companions alleviate or severely exacerbate human alienation. Following that, we dive into the terrifying reality of algorithmic surveillance. Skipping week eight for reading week, week nine directly tackles algorithmic bias, exploring how historically prejudiced training datasets structurally reinforce inequality. Week ten will cover disinformation, deepfakes, and the collapse of public trust in shared objective reality. Week eleven will confront the massive, often ignored environmental sustainability crises caused by AI data centers and resource extraction. Finally, in week twelve, we will explore AI Governance, critiquing models like the UK's regulatory approach and asking if current ethical frameworks designed by international organizations are actually doing anything to constrain corporate power, or if we need radical new alternatives. Week 13 will be strictly reserved for project consultations, so there will be no seminar that final week. I have kept you roughly ten minutes over our scheduled time today, so I apologize for that.     我们所面临的未来，无疑将比大型科技亿万富翁们所承诺的那些完美乌托邦要混乱得多、多样化得多，也复杂无限倍。没有任何一个单一的魔法算法能够解决我们所有的社会政治危机。我们的社会以前也经历过这种完全一样的混乱的技术剧变——我们在蒸汽动力、铁路网络、电报、广播和电视的发展中都见过。这正是为什么这门课程被命名为《AI、媒体与社会转型》（AI, Media, and Social Transformations）。我们来这里不是为了教你们应对未来工作所需的具体提示词工程（prompt engineering）。我们在这里是为了批判性地分析AI是如何积极地重构人类和机构沟通、传递信息以及执行社会规则的方式的。已故的杰出美国历史学家和社会学家詹姆斯·贝尼格（James Beniger）在1986年写了一本非凡的著作，名为《控制革命》（The Control Revolution）。这不是本模块的必读材料，但我强烈推荐。他提出了一个令人沮丧但至关重要的论点：在工业革命期间，当蒸汽机和铁路等爆炸性的新技术猛烈地改变了经济的物理运作时，它造成了一场巨大的控制危机。货物的移动速度超过了人类认知的追踪能力。为了管理这种令人恐惧的不确定性，社会被迫疯狂地发明庞大的新官僚机构和信息技术——比如电报——仅仅是为了维持控制并协调物质资本的流动。今天，我们正在经历一场类似的控制危机。AI就像电力或铁路一样，正在推动着前所未有的社会剧变。因此，国家和公民社会必须迅速发明新形式的官僚、法律和基础设施治理，仅仅是为了管理这种混乱。AI不仅是你个人使用的工具；它是一种正在从根本上重塑国家、法律和人类互动整个架构的基础设施。
